My last state was the first one. It ended in Rockford.

By Sean Patrick Phelps October 2026 7 min read

Delaware license plates call it the First State. For me, it was the last one.

After 49 states and Puerto Rico, my final stop was Wilmington. I wound up in Rockford Park, where a stone tower more than a century old sits on the highest ground in the city.

I grew up in Rockford, Illinois.

If you don’t know it, Rockford spent years on the wrong kind of national list. Using FBI crime data, outlets ranked it anywhere from the 5th to the 11th most dangerous city in America for its size. That isn’t the whole story of my hometown, and I’ll defend the place to anyone. But it’s the story the lists told, and a lot of kids there grew up hearing it about themselves.

I always wanted to see the rest of the country, and then the rest of the world. Fifty states and one territory later, the road dropped me in a park with my hometown’s name on it, in the state that calls itself first.

It took two decades. The early miles were business travel. Later, once I had a family, they were trips with my wife and kids, collecting states one at a time.

Here’s what the miles in between taught me.

01You can start where you are, and when you are

I grew up around business owners, and I wanted to be just like them. Almost all of them started in their forties. So I assumed that was the rule: put in your years first, and maybe someday you get your shot.

Then I met James Lawrence, the Iron Cowboy, a man who completed 50 full-distance triathlons in 50 states in 50 days. He didn’t wait for the right age, the right conditions, or permission. Meeting him inspired me more than almost anyone I found on the road, and it broke the rule I grew up believing.

The map is bigger than two coasts, too. The usual story about building something big goes through San Francisco or New York, but every state has a main street, a customer base, and someone building something real on it. That’s the whole idea behind the 50 State Startup. You don’t need the right zip code, or the right birthday, to begin. You need a customer, a plan, and the nerve to start before you feel ready.

02Where you start is a fact, not a forecast

Kids from cities that land on those lists hear a forecast early. Sometimes it’s said out loud. Usually it’s just in the air.

On a visit home to see my parents, I went rucking along the Rock River, the way I usually do, with a weighted pack and an audiobook. The route took me past the house on Ridge Avenue where I lived when I was born. It’s boarded up now, close to condemned.

A little farther on, the bike path rounds a bend lined with highlights of remarkable people from the Rockford area.

In my headphones was a book about the founding of Netflix. A new chapter opened with a line from a mountaineer: “Getting to the top is optional. Getting down is mandatory.”

I didn’t know the story behind it, but it stopped me. I didn’t know if I’d made it to the top in my life yet. I did know I’d made it back to the bottom.

So I called my parents, who now live in a custom home they built on a golf course in a great part of town. They made it from the bottom too. We cried for a minute on the phone.

Then I looked up. At that exact moment, I was standing at a sign honoring Ed Viesturs, the mountaineer behind the quote, and the first American to climb all fourteen of the world’s 8,000-meter peaks. He grew up in Rockford.

The rankings described a place. They never decided what anyone from that place could do. Ed Viesturs came from there. My parents climbed out from there. So did I. The difference was never the starting point. It was refusing to underestimate ourselves.

Where you start is a fact. It isn’t a forecast.

03Experience is the best teacher there is

I believe experience is the single greatest form of education, and 50 states made the case better than any classroom could. You can read about a place, or you can stand in it, eat with the people who live there, and see what they’re proud of.

The moment that changed how I saw this country came slowly, then all at once. After two decades of meeting people, students, colleges, and communities from every corner of the U.S., it finally hit me. The scenery is different. The accent is different. Just about everything else is the same.

People want the same things for their kids, carry the same pride in their hometowns, and respond to the same honest effort. The country is far less divided up close than it looks on a screen.

The first state, last

A kid from Rockford, Illinois, who always wanted to see everything, finished the list in the First State, in a park named Rockford. You can call it coincidence. I call it a reminder.

You carry where you’re from to every place you go. It isn’t a weight. It’s the reason the view from the top means something.

What’s next: 100 more, and 50 in 50

Finishing the 50 states didn’t end the list. It started a longer one.

I keep a written plan I call the Walter Mitty 100: one hundred experiences mapped across the next thirty years, each with a target year and the names of who’s coming along. Most of them are with my family. The Inca Trail into Machu Picchu. Kilimanjaro and the great migration across the Serengeti. Everest Base Camp. My grandfather’s hometown of Sambuca di Sicilia.

Writing it down is the whole trick. A someday list is a wish. A list with years and names on it is a plan you can hold yourself to.

One item is bigger than the rest. At some point in my life, I plan to launch a business in all 50 states in 50 weeks. One state, one new business, every week. It’s the 50 State Startup taken literally, and the loudest way I know to prove you can start where you are, anywhere on the map.

Before any of that, one step for you: write down the first place you want to see that isn’t home, then put a date next to it. That’s how 50 starts.
